The prototype

One press, two screens

A controller read, sometimes called a poll, asks the pad what you’re holding. The official read gives Smash the input that counts for a frame. An extra read between official ones can give the other player a head start.

0fSmash can use your official input on the current frame. Reading the controller and drawing the picture still take time.

1fYour official input is assigned one game frame later. At 60 frames per second, that adds about 16.7 milliseconds.

RollbackWhile your input is traveling, the opponent’s emulator can guess. If it guesses wrong, it goes back and replays the affected frames.

Your own screen still follows your official read at your chosen delay. The prototype changes the opponent’s first guess. How often that visibly helps in a real match is the part we still need to measure.

Which controllers can send hints?

Both peers advertise support in a 1v1 lobby match. Cached raphnet adapters reuse the samples they already take. For a direct adapter, RMG-K learns the timing of this game’s regular reads. If they are fast and there is a clear gap before the next one, it tries an occasional extra read. After four clean trials it can try once per frame. A slow or failed trial, or a measured overlap with game I/O, triggers a cooldown. This happens automatically.

Either peer can receive hints. Button hints cover shield, attack and C-button jumps. In Smash 64 (U or J) and Smash Remix, the same reads can also catch a stick flick. Moving 56 units left or right crosses the dash threshold. Moving 45 units up crosses the running-jump threshold, and 53 reaches the normal stick-jump threshold. The opponent gets the measured stick position. Smash decides whether your fighter can move.

Where the four proposals stand

#1

Read your controller later

A press just after the normal cutoff could make the current frame. But sending the official input later gives the other player less time to draw your move correctly.

Removed from the branchYour official read stays in its usual place. The display change can show that same input earlier.

#2

Give a missing input a chance to arrive

Send your official input. If a remote input is missing, use up to 2 ms of spare time and check again at intervals of up to 1 ms. An input that arrives in time can put the opponent’s shield on the first picture.

On for testingGekko proceeds immediately when all inputs are ready or a correction is already due. Recent slow frames reduce the time available to wait.

#3

Make rollback resimulation cheaper

If a correction goes back four frames, the emulator restores a saved state and plays those frames again. Finishing before the next picture is due can spare both players a hitch. The corrected move can still look different.

Hidden drawing is already reducedWith framebuffer emulation off, GLideN64 skips eligible drawing on hidden frames. The launcher’s -DrawHiddenFrames switch lets you measure that saving. The open question is how much time restoring state and replaying the game still take on frames that miss their display deadline.

#4

Make a better guess about the stick

Today, a missing stick reading usually means “keep holding the last direction.” If recent official readings show a player letting go, easing toward neutral might produce a smaller correction. A bad guess during a recovery could also show the wrong drift.

What’s leftCompare both guesses on recorded dashes, reversals and recoveries in each game. This can work from past readings even when the opponent sends no fresh stick hint.

Software-only evaluation

What to test next

  1. Check the display gain at one delay. Use the same game, opponent, input sequence and each player’s delay for Baseline and Display. Compare the median, 95th and 99th percentile time from a local read to submission of the picture for its assigned frame.
  2. Count corrections on both screens. Compare Display with DisplayWait, then DisplayWait with Combined to add hints. Record how often a correction occurs and how many frames it reaches back. Check footage of shields, hits or recoveries for the size of the visible change. A replay count alone cannot tell you that.
  3. Try one frame less delay. Once the combined version improves those results at the same delay, lower one player’s delay by a frame. Check whether the frequency and size of corrections on both screens stay within what the players accepted at the old setting.

To check a direct adapter, repeat the same Combined matchup at the same delays. On the player whose adapter you are checking, run once normally and once with -NoDirectHintProbes. After each game, open View → Log and find Raphnet direct hints. It reports probes, overlaps, the longest overlap in microseconds, and official reads over 2 ms. Use the pacing trace to check the slowest input submissions and late pictures. On the other PC, compare hint accuracy with -VerboseStats and compare rollback frequency and depth. A run with zero probes gives no overlap rate.

Repeat at the delays people actually choose for nearby and distant opponents. The players can choose different delays from each other. Cover stock Smash and Remix, including a connection with uneven packet arrival times.

Where to find the measurements

Enable pacing trace logging and read local_sample_to_swap_us alongside sampled_delay_frames in the frontend CSV. Skip values of −1, which indicate an unmatched sample or an unmeasured swap. The interval starts when the emulator’s official read completes and ends at OpenGL swap submission. Adapter sample age and monitor scanout are outside it. The trace also records rollback loads, frames replayed and late presentation. To isolate stick hints, compare two Combined runs at the same delay, one with -ButtonHintsOnly. With -VerboseStats, stick_range_helped_frames and stick_range_hurt_frames count frames where hints corrected or spoiled the opponent’s guess about these stick ranges. A smaller error can still require rollback if the exact stick position differs.
